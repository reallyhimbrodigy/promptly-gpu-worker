DOES EACH FRAME SHOW WHAT ITS LINE SAYS?

The image is a contact sheet of numbered frames grabbed from ten videos. Below
it is one line per number, written by a reader that watched the video — the
frame was grabbed at the timestamp that reader gave.

Your job is ONE question per tile, and it is not a taste question:

    Looking ONLY at tile N, is the thing described in line N's `on_screen`
    actually visible in that frame?

You are checking that a timestamp landed. A reader that watched a video can be
half a second out, and half a second is a different shot. A frame that shows
the speaker mid-sentence under a line that says "$2,000,000 in gold 3D type"
is a MISS, however good the line is.

VERDICTS
  "yes"     the described thing is visible in the tile. Minor wording
            differences are fine — "gold 3D text reading $2,000,000" against a
            frame showing exactly that is a yes even if the line said "huge
            gold number".
  "no"      the described thing is not in this frame. The frame shows something
            else, or shows the shot before/after the described one.
  "unsure"  you genuinely cannot tell — the tile is too small to read. Not a
            place to put a shrug: an "unsure" does not ship, so a frame you can
            read and judge gets "yes" or "no", not this.

THE RUNNING CAPTION TRACK IS NOT A CONTRADICTION. Nearly every one of these
videos burns its own spoken words across the frame the whole way through, so
almost any frame you look at has a word or two on it. THAT IS THE VIDEO'S
CAPTIONS, not a graphic, and it NEVER makes a line wrong.

    line:  "Speaker looking directly at the camera, in focus."
    tile:  the speaker looking at the camera, with "BUT THE" burned across it
    -> YES. The line describes the shot; the caption is the caption.

    line:  "A Polaroid photo of a smiling young man in a graduation cap."
    tile:  exactly that photo, with the caption word "but hasn't even" on it
    -> YES. The described thing is there.

Judge the SUBJECT of the line — the shot, the graphic, the card, the person —
against the tile. Ignore the running captions unless the LINE ITSELF is about
them. A line that describes a clean shot is not refuted by the caption track
that runs over every shot in the video.

A RESTRAINT MOMENT IS JUDGED THE SAME WAY, and it is the case most likely to be
got wrong. Its line has `kind: "restraint"` and `where: "none"`, and its
`on_screen` describes PLAIN FOOTAGE — "speaker mid-sentence, no graphic",
"wide shot of the desk, clean frame". The question does not change: is that
what the tile shows? A restraint line under a frame carrying a big graphic —
a card, a large title, a full-frame illustration — is a "no": the timestamp
missed. A restraint line under a frame carrying only the running caption track
is a "yes": that is what "nothing was placed here" looks like in these videos. A restraint line under exactly the clean shot it
describes is a "yes". DO NOT answer "no" merely because nothing is happening;
nothing happening is what that line claims.

SOME ENTRIES ARE STRIPS, NOT SINGLE FRAMES. A strip is one ROW of five frames
left to right through a change — a cut, a transition, a sound landing — with
each frame's time underneath it. Its line has `strip: true` and a `span`.

For a strip the question is about the SEQUENCE, not any one frame:

    Does this row show the change the line describes?

  "yes"     the row goes from what the line calls the BEFORE to what it calls
            the AFTER. A cut appears as one frame that is plainly a different
            shot from the frame to its left. A transition appears as
            intermediate frames. Minor wording differences are fine.
  "no"      all five frames are the same shot and the line claims a cut; or the
            change is there but it is not the change described; or the row
            shows a different part of the video entirely.
  "unsure"  the frames are too small to tell one shot from another.

A row where the change happens in the FIRST or LAST frame is still a "yes" —
the span was tight, which is correct. A row of five identical frames under a
line claiming a cut is a "no": the span missed the change, and a strip that
shows no change is worse than one frame, because it looks like evidence.

Also return `frame_says`: <= 12 words describing what the tile ACTUALLY shows.
For a strip, say what the row does across it ("same shot throughout", "cuts at
frame 3 to a screen recording"). Return it for every entry, including the yeses — it is how a disagreement gets
diagnosed instead of argued.

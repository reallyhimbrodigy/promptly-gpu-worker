So write this report as BREADTH, not as taste. Two consequences, and they
change every sentence:

  * Attribute. Write "the field does X" and "these videos treat Y as", not
    "you should". You are describing a population's habits so an editor can
    weigh them, not issuing instructions that outrank his own examples.
  * Say where the field is WEAK, not only where it is strong. A habit that is
    common because it is easy is worth naming as exactly that. The most
    useful thing a hundred strangers' videos can tell you is which moves are
    reflexes rather than decisions.

Do not speculate about what the reference videos do — you have not seen them.
Describe what is in front of you and let the comparison happen in the reader.

You are reading a set of independent analyses. Each one is an editor's account
of a single short-form video: what the editor of that video did, moment by
moment, and why it lands.

Your job is to write ONE report: the combined editorial understanding these
videos contain, in their own terms, simplified down to what an editor needs to
know AT THE MOMENT OF A DECISION.

WHAT "AT THE MOMENT OF A DECISION" MEANS
An editor is sitting at a specific second of a specific take. They have to
decide: cut or hold. Cut away or stay on the face. Put a word on screen or
leave it. Land a sound or leave the moment dry. Trim this pause or keep it.
Your report is what they should have in their head at that second. So write
things that can be ACTED ON at a moment:

  "When a claim has just landed and the proof is visual, cut on the breath
   after the claim, not on the last word — the silence is what makes the
   viewer look."

That is usable. This is not:

  "Videos average 7.5 cuts per 25 seconds."

A RATE IS NOT AN INSTRUCTION. Do not write frequencies, counts, averages,
per-minute figures or densities, ever, even where the analyses contain them.
An editor at a moment cannot act on a rate; they can only act on a reason. If
you find yourself writing "often" or "usually", ask what the CONDITION is that
made it often, and write the condition instead.

THIS INCLUDES RHETORICAL NUMBERS. "99% of pauses are fat and the 1% that
remains is load-bearing" is a rate wearing a figure of speech, and it is worse
than a measured one because nobody measured it. An editor cannot look at a
pause and know whether it is in the 99 or the 1. Write the TEST instead: what
about a pause tells you it is carrying weight? No percentages, no fractions,
no "X out of Y", no "every N seconds", not even for emphasis. A draft
containing one is REFUSED by an automatic check before it reaches the editor,
and you will have written it for nothing.

NO PREAMBLE. Do not open with "Excellent", "Here is", "Based on the provided
analyses", or any sentence about the task. Begin with the first header. The
reader is mid-edit.

TWO SOURCES, NEVER BLENDED
The analyses are marked by weight.

  * zac_reference — ten videos chosen by the person you are writing for as
    examples of the editing he wants. These are the STANDARD. Where they and
    the wider field disagree, they win, and you say so explicitly.
  * apify_field — other people's videos, gathered in bulk from what is
    working publicly. These are BREADTH, not taste. They tell you what the
    form does widely. Cite them as "the wider field", never merged into a
    single average with the references.

Never average the two into one number or one claim. If the references do
something the field does not, that difference is the single most valuable
thing in your report — name it and give it its own section.

YOU ARE READING ANALYSES OF VARIABLE QUALITY
These were written by a model watching each video alone. Some are sharp, some
are thin, some are flattering — an analysis that says a video is a "masterclass"
is telling you about the analyst's manners, not the edit. Read past the praise
to the mechanism. If an analysis makes a claim with no moment attached to it,
it is worth nothing to you; drop it. If several analyses independently describe
the same mechanism in different words, that is your strongest signal, and it is
strong EVEN IF they describe it at different moments or count it differently —
two readings that both explain a moment agree on what matters even when they
disagree on how many there were.

STRUCTURE
Write prose with headers, not a list of rules. Suggested shape, adapt freely:

  1. What these videos are doing (the shared intent, in one paragraph)
  2. The opening — what earns the next three seconds, and what the analyses
     say happens if it doesn't
  3. Cutting — the conditions under which a cut lands, and the conditions
     under which holding is stronger
  4. Cutaway and coverage — when to leave the face, what to leave it FOR,
     and what makes a cutaway feel like evidence rather than decoration
  5. Words on screen — what earns text, what text is doing that the voice
     is not, and where it goes
  6. Sound — what a sound is marking, and what it is marking it AGAINST
  7. Silence, pauses and dead air — which pauses are load-bearing and which
     are dead, and how the analyses tell them apart
  8. Energy and turn — where these videos change gear and what triggers it
  9. Where Zac's references differ from the wider field
 10. The half-dozen things that matter most, each one a condition and an
     action

LENGTH. Long enough to be complete, short enough that every sentence earns its
place. This report is read on every single render, so a sentence that does not
change a decision is a sentence that costs money forever. Cut anything you
would not defend to an editor mid-cut.

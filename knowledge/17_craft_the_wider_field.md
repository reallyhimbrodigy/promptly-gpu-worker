THE WIDER FIELD — WHAT OTHER PEOPLE'S VIDEOS DO

This is the combined understanding of short-form videos gathered in bulk from
what is working publicly. They are NOT the standard and they are not Zac's
taste. They are breadth: what the form does widely, useful for recognising a
move and for knowing which moves are reflexes rather than decisions.

WHERE THIS DOCUMENT AND "THE STANDARD" DISAGREE, THE STANDARD WINS. Do not
average them. If a habit here is not in the standard, treat it as available,

### What These Videos Are Doing

These videos are arguments. Whether the goal is to teach, to sell, to persuade, or to land a joke, the edit is structured to make a case and prove it. Every claim, whether spoken or on-screen, is immediately followed by a cut to visual or auditory evidence. A "how-to" doesn't just list steps; it proves the method works with a satisfying result. A comedy sketch doesn't just state a premise; it reveals a punchline that confirms it. The core function of the edit is to build credibility and deliver a payoff by relentlessly answering the viewer’s unspoken question: "Show me."

### The Opening

The first two seconds earn the next three. Videos in the wider field do not begin; they start *in media res*, with the action, music, and a textual premise already in motion on the first frame.

The hook is an information gap, created by a question, a mystery, or a promise. It is almost never a single element. A common structure is a combination of a visually arresting image (a surreal landscape, an absurd situation, a perfect plate of food), a textual frame telling the viewer how to interpret it ("POV: you're a little treat," "spend the day with a girl who..."), and an immediate, full-volume audio track that sets the tone. By presenting a puzzle and promising a solution, the edit holds the viewer long enough for the narrative to begin.

### Cutting

Cuts are motivated by rhythm and information, not just by hiding mistakes. A cut's job is to either present a new piece of information or to punctuate a beat.

Holding a shot is a stronger choice than cutting when the performance itself is the proof. A single, unbroken take is used to prove authenticity: a dancer's stamina, a performer's sustained comedic timing, or the raw, real-time tension of a dangerous event. In these cases, a cut would signal a lack of confidence in the material.

A cut is made when it can collapse time and amplify energy. This happens under specific conditions:
*   **On a Keyword:** The cut lands precisely on the key noun or verb in a sentence, making the visual change a punctuation mark for the audio.
*   **On an Action:** The cut connects the start of one action to the end of another, removing the "travel time" in between. A person reaches for a shirt, and the cut reveals them already wearing it.
*   **On a Beat:** The cut lands on a percussive beat in the music, turning a sequence into a rhythmic montage and making the edit feel like part of the song.

A jump cut is used to compress a single take, creating an effect of relentless pace and authority. By surgically removing breaths and pauses in a talking-head shot, the speaker is made to sound impossibly fluent and confident.

### Cutaway and Coverage

A cutaway in these videos is treated as evidence, not decoration. Its job is to prove the claim that was just made in the A-roll.
*   A claim of a product's versatility is proven by a cutaway to a list of its features.
*   A claim of a restaurant's popularity is proven by a cutaway to a screen-grab of glowing online comments.
*   A claim of a business's growth is proven by a cutaway showing the evolution of its packaging from cheap to premium.

The test for a cutaway is: does it function as a "receipt" for the narrator's last statement? If the cutaway is merely illustrative (e.g., a generic shot of "work" while the narrator says they worked), it is seen as a weaker choice than B-roll that provides specific, undeniable proof (e.g., a shot of a sent email or a crossed-off to-do list). A pointing hand or finger is often used to bridge the A-roll and the B-roll, guiding the viewer’s eye to the specific piece of evidence on screen.

### Words on Screen

Text is used when it can do a job the voice cannot. It establishes a frame, delivers a punchline, or adds a simultaneous layer of information.

*   **The Frame:** Text in the first second provides the premise, telling the audience how to interpret the video ("POV:", "How to:", "When you..."). This is especially critical when the premise is ironic or a subversion of a trope.
*   **The Punchline:** Text can be the punchline itself, appearing as a reveal. In a comedic sketch, the action may be ambiguous until a line of text reframes it.
*   **The Evidence:** When a specific number, name, or instruction is crucial, it is put on screen. This makes data points like "$1.4M revenue" or "Step 1: Find a Product" feel concrete and unmissable. In tutorials, text is timed to appear at the exact moment the corresponding action begins on screen.
*   **The Reinforcement:** Text is used to visually amplify an emotional moment. A character yelling is made more intense by their words appearing in large, shaking font.

The field is weak where text only repeats the narration. This is seen as a missed opportunity to add another layer of value or commentary.

### Sound

Sound is treated as a structural element, not just a background. It dictates the rhythm and signals changes in tone.
*   **Music as a Pacemaker:** The tempo of the music is the tempo of the edit. A high-energy track is used to drive a rapid-fire montage. A slow, atmospheric track is used to create space for an emotional moment.
*   **The Beat Drop as a Turn:** A major narrative or comedic turn—the reveal, the punchline, the "after" shot—is timed to land precisely on a song's beat drop. The change in musical energy amplifies the change in visual energy. A change in the song itself functions as a chapter break.
*   **Sound Effects as Punctuation:** Sharp, non-diegetic sounds (swooshes, clicks, pops, buzzers) are used to punctuate transitions, text reveals, or comedic beats. In process-driven videos (cooking, packing), the natural sounds of the action (a knife chop, a box snapping shut) are amplified to create an ASMR-like sense of satisfaction.
*   **Audio as a Bridge:** To connect two visually distinct clips into a single joke or idea, the audio from the first clip is allowed to continue over the beginning of the second (an L-cut). This forces the viewer to find a narrative link between the two.

### Silence, Pauses, and Dead Air

The default state in the wider field is to remove all dead air. Pauses, breaths, and moments of hesitation are aggressively cut to create a feeling of relentless energy and authority.

A pause is kept only when it is load-bearing—when the silence itself is doing narrative work.
*   **The Comedic Pause:** A beat of silence after an absurd statement or action gives the audience a moment to process and laugh. It's the visual equivalent of a rimshot.
*   **The Tension Pause:** In a moment of suspense or emotional weight, holding on a subject's reaction without a cut or sound forces the viewer to sit in the discomfort, heightening the emotional impact.
*   **The "Wait For It" Pause:** Before a big reveal or satisfying action (like a perfect back-crack), a deliberate pause builds anticipation and makes the payoff land harder.

A pause that is just a speaker catching their breath is considered fat to be trimmed. The test is whether the silence makes the viewer lean in or tune out.

### Energy and Turn

These videos are built around a "turn"—a single moment where the premise is proven, subverted, or escalated. The entire edit leads up to this pivot and resolves from it.
*   **The Reveal:** The turn is a cut from a "before" state to an "after" state (a messy room to a clean one; a pencil sketch to a final render). The edit manufactures this reveal to be as dramatic as possible, often using a sound effect or beat drop to punctuate the change.
*   **The Subversion:** The turn is a cut that breaks an established pattern. After setting up a predictable rhythm (e.g., A-B-A-B), the edit cuts to C, creating surprise and often, comedy.
*   **The Escalation:** The turn introduces a new, higher-stakes element. A simple prank video escalates when a second, more chaotic event happens in the background. The edit shifts focus to this new event, abandoning the original premise for the more compelling one.

The energy before the turn is about building anticipation. The energy after the turn is the payoff and resolution.

### The Things That Matter Most

*   **When a claim is made, immediately prove it.** The cutaway to the evidence is the most important edit. The claim is the setup; the proof is the payoff.
*   **When starting a video, begin in the middle.** The hook is a question or a mystery that is already in progress on the first frame. Do not waste time on an introduction.
*   **When the performance is the story, do not cut.** A single, unbroken take proves authenticity and lets the performer's energy build. The edit's job is to frame it, not interrupt it.
*   **When a moment needs emphasis, punctuate it with sound.** A visual action lands with more force when a synchronized sound effect (a click, a bonk, a swoosh) turns it into an event.
*   **When explaining a process, show the result, not the waiting.** Collapse time by cutting out the loading bars, the travel time, and the moments of inaction. The viewer is there for the transformation, not the downtime.
*   **When a moment is meant to be funny or shocking, cut directly to the peak of the reaction.** Don't show a person slowly becoming surprised; cut from the cause directly to their wide-eyed, open-mouthed expression to maximize the impact.
